How Heyworth Homeowners Can Extend Water Heater Life

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is routine maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, test the relief valve annually to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which limits scalding risk and cuts down on scale. Tankless owners should plan on descaling every year if their water is hard.

Age matters too. Most tank water heaters run 8 to 12 years, tankless units often reach 20 with regular descaling, and heat pump models typically run 10 to 15. If your heater is past that window and showing warning signs such as discolored hot water, rumbling during heating, dampness under the tank, or showers that go cold sooner, a planned replacement is nearly always less expensive and far less stressful than an emergency one with water on the floor.

The sooner you call about a hot water problem, the smaller the repair tends to be. A sediment layer that's just starting to rumble can often be fixed with a flush, yet that same buildup, ignored for a few years, can create hot spots on the tank floor and burn out the lower element on an electric model, forcing a replacement much sooner. Save our number in your contacts now so help is one call away as soon as something sounds or looks wrong. A quick call saves time, money, and cold showers.

Hot Water Headaches Heyworth Homeowners Face

Water heaters in and around Heyworth deal with a familiar set of stresses. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, minerals can be part of the picture: calcium and magnesium settle to the bottom of a tank as sediment, insulating the water from the burner, and that's what causes the popping and rumbling many homeowners hear during a heating cycle. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, which is why annual descaling matters so much where the water is hard.

Equipment age plays a big role too. Once the protective anode rod is gone, usually somewhere in the second half of a tank's life, the tank begins corroding from within, and a drip from the bottom seam often shows up next. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, natural gas and propane units in some homes and electric in others, which means we service both sides: thermocouples, burners, and venting on gas units, and heating elements and thermostats on electric ones.

Every house is set up a little differently. A home on a private well may see more sediment and sometimes a sulfur smell in the hot water, which a different anode rod often cures. A tankless unit in hard water needs annual descaling. And an older install getting a new heater often needs code items that weren't required when it went in: an expansion tank, a properly routed relief valve line, a drain pan, or strapping where required. All of these is fixable, and most are much cheaper to address early.
